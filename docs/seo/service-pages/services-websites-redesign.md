## Signs it is time to redesign

**It fails on a phone.** The site does not work on a phone, or it does, but you have to pinch and zoom.

**It loads slowly.** It takes 4 seconds to load on 5G, and most of your visitors are on a phone.

**It looks like 2018.** The design was modern then. The person who built it has moved on and the plugins have not been updated since.

**The business changed.** You dropped a service, added two, moved cities, and the homepage still describes the company you ran three years ago.

**You hide the link.** The most common reason we hear is simpler: the owner is embarrassed to send it.

**Traffic without calls.** Search Console shows visits and the phone stays quiet. The site is being found and failing to convince.

## What a website redesign changes

**Everything the visitor sees.** New copy that says what you do now. A layout built around one clear next step. Pages that load fast on a phone. A page for every service you sell, so each one can rank on its own. Forms that go to your inbox or your [CRM](/services/crm) instead of a dead mailbox.

**Most of what Google reads.** The structure underneath: clean titles, one heading hierarchy, schema markup, and plain question and answer sections that AI assistants can quote.

**What stays.** The URLs that rank, the sentences that rank, and the reviews and photos that are yours. A redesign replaces what fails and keeps what works, which is why the audit comes before the design.

## What we check before we quote

Most website redesign services start with a mood board. We start with Search Console. Before the proposal we run the current site through it and pull three lists. Every URL that has earned impressions in the last 16 months. Every URL with links from other sites pointing at it. Every page that is broken, duplicated, or thin. Then the mechanical checks: mobile rendering, load speed on a throttled connection, missing titles, headings that skip levels, forms that go nowhere. The third source is analytics: which pages get visited, where visitors leave, and which forms ever get filled in. A contact page nobody reaches is a navigation problem, not a copy problem, and the audit tells the two apart.

Those lists decide the shape of the project. A site with 40 pages and 6 that matter is a different job from a site with 40 pages that all rank for something. You see the lists on the call, so the quote makes sense to you.

## What a redesign must not break

This is the part most redesigns get wrong, and it is why "we redesigned the site and traffic fell off a cliff" is such a common story.

An old site has history. Some of its pages rank, some have links from other sites, and some get impressions you never noticed. A redesign that changes every URL throws that history away. Google treats the new site as a stranger.

So the URLs that earn attention either stay exactly the same or get a single hop redirect to their replacement. No chains, no 404s on pages that rank, no changed headings on a page sitting at position two. Google publishes its own rules for [site moves with URL changes](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes), and we follow them.

If a page already ranks in the top three, we leave its content alone and redesign around it. You cannot beat first place by editing first place.

## Copy before layout

The words are usually why the old site failed, so they get rewritten first, from a call with you and from the questions your customers ask before they buy. The first screen answers the four questions a visitor arrives with: what, for whom, where, and what happens next. Every service on its own page with a plain answer at the top. Only then does the design start, because the layout has to carry the message.

If your old copy has sentences that rank, they survive the rewrite. The audit tells us which ones.

## Redesign or repair

Not every old site needs a rebuild. Sometimes the structure is fine and the copy is the problem. Sometimes the site is fine and the Google Business Profile is what needs work. We look at what you have before quoting, and if a cleanup is all it needs, the proposal is for a cleanup. If it needs more than that, the proposal says why, with the pages and the numbers pulled from your own Search Console.

## Launch day and the month after

The redirect map goes live with the site, not the day after. We resubmit the sitemap, request indexing on the pages that changed, and watch Search Console for the two things that signal trouble: 404s on URLs that used to earn impressions, and pages Google now considers duplicates. For the first month we check weekly. Rankings usually wobble for a few weeks after a move and then settle; if a page drops and stays down, we find out why before anyone panics.

After that, the site is yours to run. If you want the rankings to keep climbing rather than hold, that is our [SEO service](/services/seo), and the rebuilt site is ready for it.

## Questions people ask about redesigns

### How much does a website redesign cost?

A redesign is priced like a new build, because it usually is one: a single fixed price after one call. What changes it is how many pages rank and have to be preserved, how much content has to migrate, and whether the new site needs booking, a store, or custom functionality. Redirect mapping and the Search Console checks are part of the number, never billed on top.

### Will I lose my Google rankings?

Not if the migration is done properly. We keep the URLs that rank, redirect the ones that must change with a single 301 each, keep the headings on pages that already rank well, and check Search Console the day we launch. Rankings usually settle within a few weeks. The redesigns that lose traffic are the ones that changed every URL and never looked.

### How long does a website redesign take?

4 to 6 weeks for most sites, up to 8 for larger sites with custom functionality. Content migration is the usual variable: 50 blog posts take longer to move than 5.

### Can we keep our current CMS?

Usually, if it is not the reason the site is slow. We work with the platform you have when it makes sense and move you when it does not. Which one it is comes out of the audit, with the reason.

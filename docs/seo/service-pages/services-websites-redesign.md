## Signs it is time to redesign

The site does not work on a phone, or it does, but you have to pinch and zoom. It takes four seconds to load on 5G. The design was modern in 2018. The person who built it has moved on and the plugins have not been updated since.

Or the business changed and the site did not. You dropped a service, added two, moved cities, and the homepage still describes the company you ran three years ago.

The most common reason we hear is simpler: the owner is embarrassed to send the link.

## What a website redesign changes

Everything the visitor sees and most of what Google reads. New copy that says what you do now. A layout built around one clear next step. Pages that load fast on a phone, because most of your visitors are on one. A page for every service you sell, so each one can rank on its own. Forms that go to your inbox or your [CRM](/services/crm) instead of a dead mailbox.

And the structure underneath: clean titles, one heading hierarchy, schema markup, and plain question and answer sections that AI assistants can quote.

## What we check before we quote

Before the proposal we run the current site through Search Console and pull three lists. Every URL that has earned impressions in the last 16 months. Every URL with links from other sites pointing at it. Every page that is broken, duplicated, or thin. Then the mechanical checks: mobile rendering, load speed on a throttled connection, missing titles, headings that skip levels, forms that go nowhere.

Those lists decide the shape of the project. A site with forty pages and six that matter is a different job from a site with forty pages that all rank for something. You see the lists on the call, so the quote makes sense to you.

## What a redesign must not break

This is the part most redesigns get wrong, and it is why "we redesigned the site and traffic fell off a cliff" is such a common story.

An old site has history. Some of its pages rank, some have links from other sites, and some get impressions you never noticed. A redesign that changes every URL throws that history away. Google treats the new site as a stranger.

So the URLs that earn attention either stay exactly the same or get a single hop redirect to their replacement. No chains, no 404s on pages that rank, no changed headings on a page sitting at position two. Google publishes its own rules for [site moves with URL changes](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes), and we follow them.

If a page already ranks in the top three, we leave its content alone and redesign around it. You cannot beat first place by editing first place.

## Copy before layout

The words are usually why the old site failed, so they get rewritten first, from a call with you and from the questions your customers ask before they buy. What you do, for whom, where, and what happens next, on the first screen. Every service on its own page with a plain answer at the top. Only then does the design start, because the layout has to carry the message and not the other way round.

If your old copy has sentences that rank, they survive the rewrite. The audit tells us which ones.

## Redesign or repair

Not every old site needs a rebuild. Sometimes the structure is fine and the copy is the problem. Sometimes the site is fine and the Google Business Profile is what needs work. We look at what you have before quoting, and if a cleanup is all it needs, that is what we will recommend. If it needs more than that, we will show you exactly why, with the numbers from your own Search Console.

## Launch day and the month after

The redirect map goes live with the site, not the day after. We resubmit the sitemap, request indexing on the pages that changed, and watch Search Console for the two things that signal trouble: 404s on URLs that used to earn impressions, and pages Google now considers duplicates. For the first month we check weekly. Rankings usually wobble for a few weeks after a move and then settle; if a page drops and stays down, we find out why before anyone panics.

After that, the site is yours to run. If you want the rankings to keep climbing rather than just hold, that is our [SEO service](/services/seo), and the rebuilt site is ready for it.

## Questions people ask about redesigns

### How much does a website redesign cost?

A redesign is priced like a new build, because it usually is one: most land between $2,500 and $6,000, with online stores and custom functionality above that. Migration work, redirect mapping, and the Search Console checks are included in that number, not billed on top. You get a fixed price after one call.

### Will I lose my Google rankings?

Not if the migration is done properly. We keep the URLs that rank, redirect the ones that must change with a single 301 each, keep the headings on pages that already rank well, and check Search Console the day we launch. Rankings usually settle within a few weeks. The redesigns that lose traffic are the ones that changed every URL and never looked.

### How long does a website redesign take?

Four to six weeks for most sites, up to eight for larger sites with custom functionality. Content migration is the usual variable: fifty blog posts take longer to move than five.

### Can we keep our current CMS?

Usually, if it is not the reason the site is slow. We work with the platform you have when it makes sense and move you when it does not. We will tell you which on the call, and why.
